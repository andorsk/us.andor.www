Soho, WestminsterAutumn, 1854One Penny

Salt, Sugar, Water

If I could carry one thing back to the 1800s, it would fit on a scrap of paper.

A story, by AndorWith a note on why

I came back with nothing in my pockets but one sentence, written on the inside of my wrist in ink that I kept going over every morning in case it faded: a litre of boiled water, six spoons of sugar, half a spoon of salt.

I had a long list once. Penicillin, the steam turbine, the germ theory, the double helix, a decent bicycle. Then I tried to imagine actually standing in London in 1854 with no money, no references and an odd accent, and the list shrank. Nobody would grow mould for me. Nobody would fund a turbine on the word of a stranger. But anyone can boil a kettle.

I arrived at the end of August, in the week the cholera came to Soho. It came the way it always did: a man well at breakfast and grey by supper, emptying out from the inside, his eyes sunk into his face, his skin cold and loose, his voice a whisper. People did not die of a fever. They died of drying out. The water in them simply left, and the salt went with it, and by the second day there was not enough of either to keep a heart going.

I took a room above a bootmaker in Poland Street, with a widow who let lodgings and had three children and a cough. By the Thursday the eldest boy, Tom, was sick. The surgeon who came was young, tired and decent. He gave the boy a little laudanum and a dose of calomel and told the mother, kindly, to prepare herself.

I asked her for her kettle.

The scrap of paperOne litre of clean water (a little short of a quart), boiled and cooled.
Six level spoons of sugar. Half a level spoon of salt.
Stir. Give it by the spoonful, a sip every minute,
and do not stop, even when it comes straight back up.

She looked at me as though I'd offered to cure him with a hymn. Sugar was dear. Water was what everyone agreed had made him ill. I said, truthfully, that I had seen it work, and that it could not hurt him more than he was already hurt. And because it was two in the morning and the surgeon had gone, she let me.

It is not dramatic. That is the trouble with it, and the glory. There is no moment where the patient sits up. There is a woman holding a spoon to a child's mouth every minute for nine hours, and a stranger boiling the next kettle, and a boy who is still alive at dawn, and still alive at dusk, and on the Saturday asks for bread.

The sugar is the clever part, though nobody in 1854 could have known why, and I could only half explain it. Salt alone, swallowed, runs straight through a cholera gut. Salt with sugar is pulled in together, the sugar carrying the salt across, the salt drawing the water after it. The gut that is pouring water out can still, it turns out, be persuaded to take some back in. It took the twentieth century a long time to find that door, and it had been open the whole time.

By the next week I had written the sentence out a hundred times, on the backs of handbills and the margins of the Morning Chronicle, and given it to anyone who would take it. Most threw it away. Some boiled water anyway, which helped on its own. A few, the desperate and the stubborn, sat up through the night with a spoon.

The young surgeon came back for the widow's middle child, and found the eldest boy eating. He asked me where I had trained. I said nowhere. He asked me why it worked. I told him what I could: that the disease killed by thirst, not by poison; that you had to put back what it took, faster than it took it; that the blood of the dying was thick and short of salt, which a doctor in Dublin had measured some twenty years before. Then I asked him whether he had heard of a man in Leith who had once put salt water straight into the veins of cholera patients, and brought some of them back from the edge.

He had heard of it. Everyone had, a little, and nobody did it any more. It had seemed a curiosity: dramatic, dangerous, and forgotten when its inventor died. The right idea had come close twice. It had simply never been made small enough to fit into a kitchen.

A few streets away, that same week, a quieter doctor was knocking on doors with a notebook, marking every death on a map, and finding that they gathered like iron filings around the pump on Broad Street. He had the handle taken off. That was the other half of the answer: keep the poison out of the water. My half was what to do once it was already in you.

I don't know how many people the scrap of paper saved. Not many, that autumn. An idea is only as fast as the people willing to try it, and I was one odd lodger in Poland Street. But it was a seed that cost nothing, needed no factory, no patent, no proof that a Victorian could have read, and could be passed on by anyone who could remember three ingredients.

The surgeon wrote it down. That was all I wanted.

A note on why

The game is simple: you can take one technology or one piece of information back to the 1800s. Most people reach for something grand, like electricity, antibiotics, flight or the computer. Those are wonderful, but almost all of them need a whole industry underneath before they're useful. A time traveller who explains the transistor to a Victorian has given them a riddle, not a tool.

I wanted something that would work the next morning, with what was already in every kitchen, and that would save the most lives per word. Oral rehydration solution is that thing.

  • The need was enormous. Cholera swept the world in repeated pandemics through the 19th century and killed millions. Untreated, severe cholera can kill up to half of the people it strikes, mostly through dehydration and salt loss.
  • The ingredients were ordinary. Boiled water, table sugar and table salt. No factory, no patent, no electricity.
  • They were close. In 1831 William O'Shaughnessy found that the blood of cholera patients had lost water and salts. In 1832 Thomas Latta in Leith injected salt solution into patients' veins, with some remarkable recoveries. The method faded after his death. What was missing was the small, strange fact that sugar lets the gut absorb salt and water, which was only understood in the 1960s.
  • It works. When oral rehydration was used in refugee camps during the 1971 Bangladesh war, cholera deaths fell from about 30 percent of patients to under 4 percent. In 1978 The Lancet called it potentially the most important medical advance of the century. It is estimated to have saved tens of millions of lives since.
  • It travels by word of mouth. Three ingredients and one instruction: keep giving it, a little at a time. It doesn't need anyone to believe the theory first.

Runners-up: washing hands between the dead-house and the delivery room (Ignaz Semmelweis was already saying it in 1847, and was ignored); boiling drinking water; and the simple idea that diseases are caused by living things too small to see. If I could whisper a second sentence, it would be that last one.

The story above is invented: the lodger, the widow, her children and the young surgeon never existed. The setting is real. In the summer of 1854 cholera killed several hundred people around Broad Street in Soho, and the physician John Snow mapped the deaths, traced them to the street's water pump, and had its handle removed.

This is history, not medical advice. Today, use ready-made oral rehydration salts where you can, follow local health guidance, and get medical care for severe diarrhoea, especially in children.
