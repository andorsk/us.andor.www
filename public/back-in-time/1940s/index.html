Spring, 1947 · A body shop in Detroit

The V-Shaped Belt

A three-point seat belt, anchored low beside the hip, halves the deaths in a crash.

The cars coming out of Detroit after the war were heavy and fast and gorgeous, and they had nothing in them to hold you. In a crash, the driver went into the steering column and the passenger went into the windscreen. Doctors had a name for the injuries.

I took a job sweeping up at a body shop, and in the evenings I drew on the back of the order slips. A belt across the hips. A second strap across the chest, from the shoulder to the opposite hip. Both meeting at one buckle, low and to the side, where you can reach it with one hand. Anchored to the floor, not the seat, so it holds even when the seat doesn't.

The foreman said there were lap belts in aeroplanes already, and look what good they did: a lap belt alone can fold you in half. That's why the chest strap matters, I said. It's a V, not a line. It catches the strongest parts of you, your pelvis and your rib cage, and lets the rest of you stop slowly.

He let me fit one into his own coupe, for a laugh. In the autumn a milk truck ran a light and hit him on the driver's side.

He walked away with a bruise in the shape of a V. He hung the belt over the shop door, and he started fitting them for customers at cost.

It took the world another twelve years and a Swedish engineer to make it standard. But one shop in Detroit had them first.

The design

  1. A lap strap and a diagonal chest strap meeting at one buckle, beside the hip.
  2. Anchored to the car's frame at three points, not to the seat.
  3. Lies across the pelvis and rib cage, not the stomach or neck.
  4. One hand to fasten. Make it a habit before the engine starts.

A note on why

Cars were killing tens of thousands of people a year in the 1940s, and the simplest fix didn't arrive until 1959. The three-point belt is just webbing, steel and geometry; nothing about it needed any technology the 1940s lacked.

It has probably saved more lives than any other single car safety invention.

  • Nils Bohlin designed the modern three-point seat belt for Volvo in 1959; Volvo made the patent freely available.
  • Wearing a lap-and-shoulder belt reduces the risk of death for front-seat car occupants by roughly 45%, according to US road-safety estimates.
  • Seat belts are estimated to have saved over a million lives worldwide.

Runners-up for this era: 'smoking causes cancer'; antibiotics for everyone; 'don't put lead in petrol'.

The foreman, the shop and the milk truck are invented. Bohlin's design and the safety figures are real. This is a story about history, not medical, legal or safety advice.
