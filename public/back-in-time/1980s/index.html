December, 1982 · A university machine room

Sign Every Message

Build identity and encryption into the internet before it scales.

In a few weeks, every machine on the network would switch to a new protocol, and the switch was going to be permanent. The engineers in the machine room were tired and cheerful. The network had a few hundred hosts, all run by people who knew each other.

Mail was the favourite thing on it. Anyone could send a message to anyone, and the protocol simply believed whoever the message said it was from. Why wouldn't it? Everyone was a colleague.

I had come back from a world where that small kindness had grown into billions of fake messages a day, where a stranger could pretend to be your bank, your boss or your mother, and where whole industries existed to guess who was real.

I didn't need to invent anything. Public-key cryptography had been published six years earlier. I asked for something boring: that every host prove who it is, every message carry a signature, and every connection be encrypted by default. Make identity a part of the plumbing, not an afterthought.

One of the engineers asked who would manage all the keys. That is the right question, I said. Start answering it now, while you all still know each other's names.

They kept the protocol as it was. But one of them started signing his mail. Then another. Then a list of them did, as a joke, and the joke was called a web of trust.

The protocol change

  1. Every host proves its identity with a key.
  2. Every message is signed by its sender.
  3. Every connection is encrypted by default.
  4. Design how keys are issued and revoked before you need it.
And for myself: a good life in the 1980s

Buy into the personal computer

Computers are moving from basements onto desks. Microsoft goes public in March 1986 at $21 a share. A modest stake in the company that put an operating system on nearly every PC would make the rest of life comfortable.

  • Buy a little Microsoft at its 1986 IPO, and hold it.
  • Learn the PC: spreadsheets, then networks.
  • Get an internet account as soon as you can.

Microsoft's initial public offering was on 13 March 1986 at $21 per share. Hindsight, not advice.

A note on why

The internet was built for a small group of people who trusted each other, and it scaled to billions who don't. Spam, phishing and impersonation trace straight back to protocols that believed whatever they were told.

This one is personal: I work on identity and trust for AI agents, and the same mistake is waiting to be made again.

  • The ARPANET switched to TCP/IP on 1 January 1983.
  • The email protocol SMTP (1982) had no built-in way to verify a sender. Ways to authenticate senders (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) were added decades later.
  • Diffie–Hellman key exchange (1976) and RSA (1977) were published before the switch.
  • The Morris worm of 1988 was one of the first major attacks to spread across the internet.
