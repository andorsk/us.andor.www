August, 1878 · Memphis, Tennessee

Cover the Water Barrels

Mosquitoes carry yellow fever and malaria. Kill them where they breed.

Everybody in Memphis had a theory. Bad air from the river. Rotten sewers. Ships from New Orleans. The rich were already leaving on every train, and the poor were burning tar barrels in the streets to clean the air.

Yellow fever comes on suddenly: a fever, aching, a few days that seem to get better, and then, for some, the yellow skin and the black vomit that gave it its name. In that summer, it came through the city like a fire.

I had one sentence. It isn't the air; it's the mosquito. And not every mosquito: the small, striped one that bites in the daytime, that lives around houses, and lays its eggs in still water: rain barrels, cisterns, puddles in a broken crock.

I paid boys a penny a barrel to put lids on water barrels, and a half-penny for a cup of lamp oil on any standing water they couldn't empty. I strung netting over the beds of the sick, so that a mosquito couldn't bite a fever patient and carry it to the next house.

The doctors said it was nonsense: fever was a miasma, everyone knew it. The neighbours said it was harmless nonsense, and let the boys do it. The street where the barrels were covered had fewer cases. Not none. But fewer.

That's the thing about a good idea in a bad year: it doesn't save everyone. It saves the people on the street where someone believed it.

The street order

  1. Cover every rain barrel and cistern.
  2. Empty pots, troughs and puddles weekly.
  3. Float a film of oil on water you can't empty.
  4. Net the beds of the sick, so mosquitoes can't carry it on.
And for myself: a good life in 1860–1879

Keep the poison out of the house

The fashionable green of Victorian wallpaper, dresses and even sweets was made with arsenic. Lead was in paint and pipes, mercury in hats and medicine. A nice life starts with not slowly poisoning yourself at home.

  • No bright green wallpaper or dyes.
  • No lead paint, lead pipes or lead-glazed pots for food.
  • Skip patent medicines; most were opium or alcohol.

Scheele's green and Paris green, both arsenic compounds, coloured wallpaper and fabrics in the 19th century. Hindsight, not advice.

A note on why

Yellow fever and malaria killed people across the Americas, Africa and Asia, and armies died of them faster than of battle. Both are spread by mosquitoes, and both could be fought with lids, nets and drainage, with no medicine at all.

It was proposed in 1881 and proven around 1900. The Panama Canal was only finished because of it.

  • The 1878 yellow fever epidemic killed roughly 20,000 people in the Mississippi Valley; Memphis alone lost over 5,000.
  • Carlos Finlay proposed in 1881 that a mosquito (now Aedes aegypti) spread yellow fever; Walter Reed's commission confirmed it in 1900.
  • Ronald Ross showed in 1897 that mosquitoes transmit malaria. Aedes aegypti breeds in containers of still water around houses.

Runners-up for this era: pasteurising milk; handwashing; quinine for everyone.

The narrator, the boys and the street are invented. The 1878 epidemic, Finlay, Reed and Ross are real. This is a story about history, not medical, legal or safety advice.
