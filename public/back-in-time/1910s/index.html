September, 1918 · Philadelphia

Cancel the Parade

This flu spreads in crowds and stale air. Cancel gatherings early; open the windows.

The parade was going to be the biggest the city had ever seen: bands, soldiers, floats, a quarter of a million people on Broad Street, all to sell war bonds. The flu had already come in with the sailors at the Navy Yard. The health director said it was under control.

I went to every newspaper on the Thursday before. I had numbers from a future they couldn't check: that this influenza would kill tens of millions of people around the world, that it killed the young and strong, that it moved through a crowd like a struck match, and that cities which shut early buried far fewer of their own.

Most editors threw me out. One, at a small paper, printed my letter on page six: put off the parade; close the theatres, the saloons, the churches for a month; open the windows of every ward and classroom; tie a gauze mask over your face in a crowd.

The parade went ahead. Within three days the hospitals were full. Within a week, the city was burying people faster than it could dig.

But a few hundred people read page six. A school principal opened every window and kept the children home. A ward sister wheeled her beds onto the hospital roof, into the cold fresh air. A family stayed in.

I couldn't stop a parade. But I could give a few hundred people a reason to stay home from it, and I like to think some of them are why some grandchildren exist.

Page six

  1. Put off the parade. Close theatres and halls early, not late.
  2. Open the windows: sick wards, schools, offices.
  3. Nurse the sick in fresh air, apart from the well.
  4. Wear a mask in crowds, and stay home if you're ill.

A note on why

The 1918 pandemic killed more people than the war it ended alongside. There was no vaccine and no antiviral, but the simple things worked: distance, air and timing.

The difference between Philadelphia and St. Louis that autumn is one of the clearest natural experiments in public health.

  • The 1918–19 influenza pandemic is estimated to have killed around 50 million people worldwide (estimates range from about 17 to 100 million).
  • Philadelphia held its Liberty Loan parade on 28 September 1918 with some 200,000 people; it suffered one of the worst death rates of any US city.
  • St. Louis closed public gatherings early in October and had a far lower peak.
  • Open-air treatment was used during the pandemic, and some hospitals reported better outcomes with it.

Runners-up for this era: penicillin ten years early; fluids and nursing care for the sick; better masks.

The narrator, the editor, the principal and the ward sister are invented. The parade, its aftermath and St. Louis's closures are real. This is a story about history, not medical, legal or safety advice.
