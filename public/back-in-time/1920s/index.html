October, 1928 · St Mary's Hospital, Paddington

Inject the Mice

Fleming's mould juice works inside the body. Test it in mice, then purify it.

The laboratory was small and cluttered and smelled of agar. On the bench was the dish everyone would one day talk about: a blue-green mould, and around it, a clear ring where the bacteria had simply died.

The scientist was pleased with it, in the way he was pleased with most odd things. He grew more of the mould, made a broth of it, and found it killed many kinds of bacteria in a dish. But the active stuff was unstable and hard to extract, and he never quite believed it would last long enough inside a sick body to matter. Within a couple of years he moved on.

I couldn't tell him I knew. I could only be a nuisance in a white coat, and say the thing nobody tried for twelve years: inject it into infected mice, and see if they live.

And then the second thing, the chemist's trick that took a team in Oxford until 1940 to find: the drug moves between water and a solvent depending on how acid the water is. Make it acid and it goes into the solvent, leaving the muck behind; make it slightly alkaline and it comes back out into clean water. Do it cold and fast, because it falls apart.

He didn't believe me. But a young assistant did, and she infected eight mice, and treated four.

Four lived.

Two experiments

  1. Infect mice with streptococci. Inject half of them with the mould broth.
  2. Count the survivors.
  3. To purify: acidify the cold broth and shake it with a solvent; then move it back into slightly alkaline water.
  4. Work cold and quickly. Dry it to keep it.

A note on why

Penicillin was discovered in 1928 and not used to treat a patient until 1941. Those thirteen years cost countless lives to infections we now treat in a week.

The missing pieces weren't luck; they were two experiments and a chemical trick. That's exactly the kind of information you can carry back.

  • Alexander Fleming observed penicillin's effect in 1928 and published in 1929, but did not develop it as a drug.
  • In 1940 Howard Florey, Ernst Chain and Norman Heatley at Oxford showed it cured infected mice; Heatley devised the back-extraction method.
  • The first patient, a police constable, was treated in 1941; he improved, then died when the supply ran out. Mass production followed during the war.

Runners-up for this era: 'leaded petrol is a poison'; insulin (1921) was arriving anyway; seat belts.

The young assistant and her mice are invented; the bench, the dish and the Oxford work are real. This is a story about history, not medical, legal or safety advice.
