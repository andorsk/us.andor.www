Winter, 1812 · A lying-in hospital in Edinburgh

Wash Your Hands

Childbed fever is carried on the doctors' hands. Wash them in chloride of lime.

The physicians came to the lying-in ward straight from the dissecting room, still smelling of it. They were good men and proud of their learning, and they could not see what was on their hands, because nothing was.

In that ward, one mother in ten did not come home. Childbed fever came on two or three days after the birth: a shivering, a hot belly, a terrible fever, and often death within the week. It went through the beds in clusters, and the doctors said it was in the air, or the season, or the women's own constitutions.

I took a post as a porter and made a nuisance of myself. I bought bleaching powder, which the linen works had been making for a dozen years, and mixed it in a basin by the ward door. Anyone who touched a mother must first wash in it, I said, until the hands smelled of it and not of the dead-house.

The doctors laughed. The midwives, who did not go to the dissecting room, already lost fewer women, and they listened. So did one junior physician, who began to keep a tally.

By the spring his tally had a shape. On the weeks the basin was used, the deaths fell to almost nothing. On the weeks it was forgotten, they came back. He showed it to his seniors. One of them was offended at the idea that a gentleman's hands were unclean.

I could not make them believe it. But I could leave the basin by the door, and a tally in a young man's notebook, and the smell of chlorine in a ward where fewer mothers died.

By the ward door

  1. A basin of water with chloride of lime (bleaching powder) mixed in.
  2. Wash before touching any mother, every time.
  3. Above all after the dissecting room.
  4. Keep a tally of deaths, and show it to everyone.

Store food for 1816

A volcano in Indonesia erupts in April 1815, and the next year has no summer: frost in June, failed harvests across Europe and New England, hunger and high prices. I'd fill the cellar in 1815, and share it.

Mount Tambora erupted in April 1815; 1816 became the 'Year Without a Summer', with snow in New England in June. Hindsight, not advice.

A note on why

Puerperal fever killed mothers for centuries in exactly the places that should have been safest: hospitals. The fix needs no theory of germs, just a basin and a habit.

It was proposed in 1795 and proven in 1847, and still resisted for decades. Information was never the bottleneck; persuasion was. That's the most useful thing to carry back: the tally.

  • Alexander Gordon of Aberdeen argued in 1795 that puerperal fever was carried to mothers by their attendants.
  • In Vienna in 1847, Ignaz Semmelweis required handwashing in chlorinated lime; deaths on the doctors' ward fell from around 10% to about 1–2%.
  • His colleagues largely rejected the finding. Bleaching powder had been manufactured since 1799.

Runners-up for this era: boiling instruments; ether; that diseases are caused by microbes.

The ward, the porter and the junior physician are invented. Gordon's treatise and Semmelweis's results are real. This is a story about history, not medical, legal or safety advice.
