1996 · A family doctor's office in Appalachia

Read the Letter

The famous 'less than 1% addiction' claim was a five-sentence letter. Treat strong opioids with great care.

The drug rep was friendly and generous with lunch. The new painkiller lasted twelve hours, she said, and it was very unlikely to be addictive: less than one percent, and there was research to prove it.

Dr Ellis was a careful doctor who had looked after the same families for twenty years. Her patients were miners and loggers with real pain, and she wanted to help them.

I asked her to do one thing: look up the research.

It took a trip to the medical library two counties over. The famous figure came from a letter to a journal in 1980: five sentences, written about hospital patients given opioids under supervision, not about people taking strong pills at home for months. It had never claimed what it was now being used to claim.

She read it twice. Then she started prescribing the new drug only when nothing else would do, in short courses, with a plan for stopping, and she kept a list of every prescription so she would notice if a patient came back too soon.

Over the next decade, the region around her became one of the worst-hit places in America. Her practice did not escape it. But her patients were far less likely to have started it in her office.

Before you prescribe

  1. Read the actual source behind a statistic.
  2. Short courses, the lowest dose, a plan for stopping.
  3. Track every prescription and early refill.
  4. Keep naloxone, the overdose reversal drug, where it's needed.

A note on why

The US opioid epidemic has killed over half a million people, and it began with prescriptions written in good faith on the strength of a misleading claim.

The information is small and checkable: what the source actually said. That's the most powerful thing one can carry back: a habit of reading the source.

  • OxyContin was approved in the US in December 1995 and launched in 1996.
  • Marketing cited an addiction rate of 'less than 1%', traced to a 1980 letter by Porter and Jick about hospitalised patients.
  • Its maker pleaded guilty in 2007 to misbranding the drug's addiction risks.
  • From 1999 to 2019, about half a million Americans died of opioid overdoses (prescription and illicit).

Runners-up for this era: mRNA; better internet security; 'the housing market will crash in 2008'.

Dr Ellis, the rep and the practice are invented. The approval, the 1980 letter, the 2007 plea and the death figures are real. This is a story about history, not medical, legal or safety advice.
