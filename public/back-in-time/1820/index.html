November, 1832 · An operating theatre in London

A Sponge of Ether

Ether vapour makes surgery painless. It was already on the shelf.

The operating theatre was built like a lecture hall: rows of students leaning over the rail, a wooden table with a groove to carry off the blood, a box of sawdust beneath. The best surgeon in London could take off a leg in under a minute, and they timed him, because speed was the only mercy he had to give.

The patient that morning was a coal-heaver with a crushed foot. Four men were waiting to hold him down.

I had brought a brown glass bottle of sulphuric ether from the apothecary's shelf, where it sat as a remedy for colic, and a sponge in a cone of folded paper. Students had been sniffing ether at parties for years; they called them frolics, and they laughed at how they bruised themselves without feeling a thing. Nobody had thought to put that together with the table.

The surgeon let me try, because he was a kind man under the hardness, and because the coal-heaver begged him to. A few drops on the sponge. The man breathed, and coughed, and breathed, and his eyes went heavy, and his hand let go of the table edge.

The surgeon worked. The coal-heaver did not scream. The students, who had seen a hundred operations, were completely silent.

Afterwards the surgeon asked me what else the apothecary's shelf had been hiding. I told him: far fewer frightened patients, and, before long, operations nobody had dared to try at all.

The ether cone

  1. A sponge in a cone of paper or cloth, held over nose and mouth.
  2. Sulphuric (diethyl) ether, a few drops at a time.
  3. Watch the breathing and the colour of the lips.
  4. Ether vapour catches fire: no candles or flames nearby.

A note on why

Ether had been made since the 1500s, and laughing gas since 1772. Humphry Davy even wrote in 1800 that nitrous oxide might be used in surgical operations. For forty more years, people were cut open awake.

Painless surgery changed what surgery could be: longer, more careful operations, and patients who didn't refuse help because they feared the table.

  • Crawford Long used ether in surgery in Georgia in 1842; William Morton demonstrated it publicly in Boston in 1846.
  • Before anaesthesia, surgeons such as Robert Liston were famous for operating in a minute or two.
  • Ether is highly flammable and needs care with dose and airway; modern anaesthesia is far safer.

Runners-up for this era: handwashing; the stethoscope (invented 1816); vaccination.

The patient, the surgeon and the morning are invented. Davy's 1800 remark, the 'ether frolics', Long and Morton are real. This is a story about history, not medical, legal or safety advice.
