July, 1885 · A tenement on the Lower East Side, New York

Heat the Baby's Milk

Milk carries the diseases that kill babies. Heat it, cool it, keep it cold.

In the summer the babies died. Everyone on the street knew it, and mothers counted their children by the summers they had survived. They called it the summer complaint: a baby with a fever and loose bowels on Monday, and a small coffin on Friday.

The milk came from the cart, ladled from an open can into a jug. It had travelled from the country in the heat, or worse, from the city dairies where cows were fed on the hot mash left over from the breweries. By evening it smelled, and the mothers diluted it with water from the hall tap.

I didn't have a refrigerator to give anyone. I had a saucepan, a thermometer, and something Pasteur had shown twenty years earlier for wine: heat kills the things you can't see.

Heat the milk until it steams and stay just short of a boil, hold it there, then stand the bottle in cold water and keep it in the coolest place you have. Scald the bottle and the nipple. Throw out what's left at the end of the day.

Mrs Kowalski on the third floor tried it because her last baby had died the summer before and she had nothing to lose. Her baby lived through August. So did her neighbour's, and her neighbour's neighbour's, once they'd seen it.

It wasn't a cure for poverty. It was a saucepan. In a city where one baby in five didn't see a first birthday, a saucepan was a great deal.

For the bottle

  1. Heat the milk until it steams, just short of boiling, for a few minutes.
  2. Cool it quickly: stand the bottle in cold water.
  3. Scald bottles and nipples every day.
  4. Keep it cold, and throw away yesterday's milk.

A note on why

In 19th-century cities, contaminated milk was one of the great killers of infants, through diarrhoeal disease and also tuberculosis from infected cows. The science (heat kills microbes) existed by the 1860s; it took until the 1890s and beyond to reach the bottle.

It's a kitchen technique, not a machine: anyone can do it tonight.

  • Louis Pasteur developed heat treatment in the 1860s, first for wine and beer.
  • In the 1850s New York's 'swill milk' scandal exposed dairies feeding cows distillery waste.
  • From 1893 the philanthropist Nathan Straus set up depots selling pasteurised milk to poor families in New York; infant deaths in the city fell over the following years.

Runners-up for this era: screens for windows; the germ theory itself; clean water filtration.

The family and street are invented. Pasteur's work, the swill milk scandal and Straus's milk depots are real. This is a story about history, not medical, legal or safety advice.
