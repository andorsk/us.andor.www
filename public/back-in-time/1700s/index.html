March, 1741 · A man-of-war rounding Cape Horn

A Chest of Lemons

Fresh citrus prevents and cures scurvy. Don't boil it.

The purser thought I was mad to waste good stowage on fruit. I had paid for the lemons myself, two chests of them bought green at Madeira, packed in sawdust, and I had made the surgeon promise that nobody would boil them into syrup.

By the third month the men began to change. It starts with tiredness, the kind that sleep does not touch. Then the gums swell and go dark and bleed, old wounds open up again, and a man who broke his arm at twenty finds the bone come apart at forty. Men died in their hammocks before they ever saw an enemy.

On our ship, a quarter-pint of lemon juice and water was given out every day to every man, and the surgeon wrote down who drank it. The ones on the starboard watch, who drank it, kept their teeth. Two men who poured theirs away, because a sailor will refuse anything on principle, were the first to sicken.

When the chests ran low, we put into an island and loaded oranges, limes, green stuff, anything that grew. The bosun called it gardening. The captain, who had started the voyage calling it nonsense, began calling it his own idea.

The trick is not the fruit; everyone had heard of fruit. The trick is to keep the juice fresh: heat ruins it, copper ruins it, time ruins it. A century of good intentions was undone by people who boiled the juice down so that it would keep.

We came home with most of the men we left with. In that century, that was the miracle.

The surgeon's order

  1. A quarter-pint of fresh lemon or orange juice, in water, every man, every day.
  2. Do not boil it. Do not keep it in copper.
  3. When the fruit runs out, put into port for fresh fruit and greens.

A note on why

Scurvy was the great killer of the age of sail: it took more sailors than storms and cannon. The cure was known, lost, found and lost again for two centuries, mostly because nobody knew why it worked or how easily it was destroyed.

The useful information isn't just 'eat citrus'. It's 'fresh citrus, kept cool, every day, and never boiled'.

  • On George Anson's voyage round the world (1740–44), most of the roughly 1,900 men who set out died, the great majority from scurvy.
  • James Lancaster's East India Company fleet used lemon juice in 1601; James Lind's 1747 trial found oranges and lemons worked best.
  • The Royal Navy only made lemon juice a standard ration in 1795. Later, boiled or lime-based juice was much less effective, and scurvy returned.
  • Scurvy is caused by lack of vitamin C, which is destroyed by heat and by long storage.

Runners-up for this era: cowpox vaccination; the marine chronometer; washing hands.

The ship, the purser, the surgeon and the captain are invented. Anson's voyage, Lancaster's lemons and Lind's trial are real. This is a story about history, not medical, legal or safety advice.
