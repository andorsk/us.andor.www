Spring, 1665 · A dairy farm in Dorset

The Milkmaid's Hands

Cowpox protects against smallpox. Scratch it into the arm.

Everyone in the valley already knew the saying, the way you know a nursery rhyme without wondering what it means: a milkmaid never has a pocked face. I had come back three hundred and sixty years to tell them what it meant.

Smallpox was the great leveller of that century. It took kings' sons and cottagers' daughters alike, and a third of those it caught it killed. The ones it spared it often scarred or blinded. In a village of two hundred, it came every few years, like bad weather.

The farm belonged to a widower with five children and forty cows. In April a few of the cows came up with blisters on their teats, and so did the hands of Bess, the girl who milked them: a few angry sores, a day of feeling poorly, and then nothing. Cowpox. Nobody worried about it.

I asked Bess if I could borrow a little of the fluid from one of her blisters. Then I asked the farmer if I could scratch it into the arms of his children.

It took a week of talking, and in the end it was Bess who persuaded him, by holding up her clear, unmarked face and saying, "I've nursed the pox twice and never took it." He went first himself, to show the children it was nothing. A light scratch with a clean needle, a smear of the fluid, a sore that came up in a week and healed in three.

From each child's sore, before it healed, I took a little fluid and passed it on to the next arm: the cook's boy, the shepherd's family, the curate, who wrote it all down in Latin because he said nobody would believe it in English.

In the autumn the smallpox came to the village, as it always did. It went through the houses on the far side of the stream. It did not go into the farm.

The curate's notebook went to a cousin in Oxford. I don't know if anyone read it. But forty-two arms in Dorset had a small round scar, and every one of them could start the chain again.

The method

  1. Find a cow, or a milker, with fresh cowpox blisters.
  2. With a clean needle, take a little fluid from a blister.
  3. Scratch it lightly into the skin of the upper arm.
  4. A week later, take fluid from that sore to protect the next person.

A note on why

I wanted something that needed nothing but what was already in the barn. Smallpox vaccination is that: the protection was walking around on milkmaids' hands for centuries before anyone used it on purpose.

It is also the only time humanity has ever eradicated a human disease. Bringing it back a hundred and thirty years early is a hundred and thirty years of smallpox that never happens.

  • Smallpox killed roughly 30% of those infected with its common form, and left many survivors scarred or blind.
  • In 18th-century Europe it is estimated to have killed around 400,000 people a year.
  • The folk belief that milkmaids were immune was right. A Dorset farmer, Benjamin Jesty, deliberately gave his family cowpox in 1774; Edward Jenner tested and published the method in 1796.
  • Vaccination (from vacca, cow) led to the eradication of smallpox, declared in 1980.

Runners-up for this era: variolation (deliberate infection with mild smallpox, already practised in Asia and Africa, but riskier); lemons for sailors; boiling water.

The farm, Bess, the farmer and the curate are invented. Arm-to-arm vaccination worked, but it could also pass on other blood-borne infections; later practice moved to calf-grown vaccine for that reason. This is a story about history, not medical, legal or safety advice.
