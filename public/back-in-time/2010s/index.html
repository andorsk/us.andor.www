2016 · An aircraft design review

One Sensor Isn't Enough

Never let one sensor command the flight controls, and tell the pilots it's there.

The new version of the airliner had bigger, more efficient engines, mounted further forward. In some situations that made the nose want to pitch up. The fix was software: a system that, if it sensed the nose was too high, would quietly trim it back down.

On paper it was elegant. In the review, someone asked which sensor it listened to. The answer was one: a small vane on the side of the nose that measured the angle of the wind.

I was a contractor in that room, invented for this story, and I had come back from a future where that vane had failed twice, on two different aircraft, and the software had pushed the nose down again and again while the crews fought it without knowing what it was. Three hundred and forty-six people.

I didn't need new technology. I needed three things on the whiteboard. Compare two sensors, and if they disagree, switch the system off. Never let it push more than once without the pilot's say. And put it in the manual and the training, so that every crew knows it exists and how to stop it.

Somebody said it would add cost. Somebody said pilot training would need simulators. Both were true.

Both were cheaper.

On the whiteboard

  1. Any automated control must use at least two sensors.
  2. If they disagree, disable the automation and alert the crew.
  3. Limit how much authority it has, and how often it can act.
  4. Tell the pilots it exists. Train them on it.

A note on why

Two crashes, five months apart, killed 346 people. The investigations found the design relied on a single sensor and that pilots had not been told about the system.

The lesson reaches beyond aviation, to any automation that acts on its own, including AI: redundancy, limits, and telling the humans what it does.

  • Lion Air Flight 610 crashed on 29 October 2018 (189 killed); Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 crashed on 10 March 2019 (157 killed).
  • Investigations found the MCAS software relied on input from a single angle-of-attack sensor, and that it was not described in pilot manuals.
  • The aircraft type was grounded worldwide from March 2019 until changes, including using two sensors, were approved.

Runners-up for this era: 'it's airborne' for 2020; mRNA; 'build trust into AI agents from the start'.

The design review and the contractor are invented. The crashes and the investigation findings are real. This is a story about history, not medical, legal or safety advice.
